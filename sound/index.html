01 / Where the sound took shape

Los Angeles.
Inside the studio.

Learning how a record feels, as well as how it sounds.

Mahmood’s musical language grew through years of hands-on work in Los Angeles recording studios. As an engineer, producer and songwriter working across American Black music, funk, R&B and hip-hop, he learned to listen inside a record: to the movement of the bass, the placement of a rhythm, the shape of a vocal and the space between parts.

He was working within that musical world during the period when hip-hop was reaching increasingly broad mainstream audiences. He experienced that change as a participant in the studio industry, watching culturally distinctive music find listeners far beyond the audience once assumed for it.

That experience remains audible in his own work: an active bass line, a shared rhythmic pocket and layers that move together while leaving room for the song.

02 / Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

A collaboration.
A wider horizon.

A firsthand encounter with music crossing cultural boundaries.

His later work with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan brought another musical world into that experience. Their collaboration on Only One joined Mahmood’s songwriting and production sensibility with Nusrat’s extraordinary vocal tradition.

This was also the wider period in which Nusrat’s music was finding broader American and international audiences, around the work of Peter Gabriel’s Real World and Rick Rubin’s American Recordings. Mahmood had his own collaboration with Nusrat within that unfolding story.

Having worked in the Black music industry as hip-hop expanded its reach, and then with Nusrat during a period of Western crossover, Mahmood had seen two different musical cultures travel beyond their expected boundaries.

The lesson he carried forward: cultural character can remain intact while the audience grows. Those encounters helped shape his confidence in a sound that carries several musical traditions within an accessible song.

03 / Inside the record

The detail is felt.
The song comes first.

The melody must stand on its own. The arrangement gives it movement, depth and emotional scale. Beneath an apparently effortless record sits a carefully constructed interplay of parts.

01 / Motion

The push and the pocket

Anticipated chord changes—“pushes”—create tension and release within repeating eight-bar vamps. Short licks, pickups and changing turnarounds keep the music moving. Every instrument shares the groove.

02 / Countermotion

Bass with a voice

Active, funk-informed bass lines bring their own melodic and rhythmic movement. They converse with the guitars and vocals, adding energy within the song’s harmonic foundation.

03 / Texture

Layers that belong together

Multiple strummed guitars, open strings, piano and electric piano spread across the stereo field. Some parts are felt more than individually heard; together they give the record its body.

04 / Emotion

A record that breathes

An expressive lead voice and constructed harmonies sit within arrangements that swell and recede. Intimate passages give way to expansive choruses. Space, clarity and dynamic contrast serve the feeling.

04 / Inside the ensemble

“He doesn’t stand
in front of the music.
He sits inside it.”

The live formation expresses the same philosophy as the recordings. Mahmood sits at the deepest point of a semicircle, between players 4 and 5, with four musicians arcing out to each side.

From within the ensemble, he can meet his musicians’ eyes and respond in real time. A cue becomes a fill. A chord push becomes a shared lift. The group can thin the texture, build a swell, hold a break or follow an improvisational response.

The audience sees the musical conversation taking place. Leadership comes from within the band.

06 / The conversation continues

The song stays fixed.
The orchestration
becomes alive.

Orchestras of the World carries this approach into a new scale of collaboration. The established melody, harmony and form provide the common ground; selected players and sections have space to respond through texture, countermelody, rhythm and dynamics.

The studio layers become live exchanges. The band’s eye contact becomes an orchestral conversation. Each ensemble can bring its own musical character to the same songs.

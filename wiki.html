Early life

Mahmood Khan’s relationship with music began early. In a 2020 interview, he recalled being drawn first to songs on television, then to piano and guitar at boarding school. A large record collection and a fascination with how records were made led him toward songwriting, production and sound engineering.

Los Angeles & the studio

Khan has said that hearing the Bee Gees’ “Too Much Heaven” made him want to understand how records were built—and eventually to become a producer. That focus led him to Los Angeles, where he found work in a recording studio and developed practical experience as an engineer and producer.

Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

While developing his voice, Khan immersed himself in South Asian classical vocal traditions, studying artists including Bade Ghulam Ali Khan, Malika Pukhraj and Aziz Mian Qawwal. When Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan visited Los Angeles for a Peter Gabriel concert, Khan spent time observing his practice and absorbing Qawwali technique, discipline and riyaz. The encounter deepened the musical vocabulary that connects Khan’s work to his Pakistani heritage.

Writing across places

His work has unfolded between Pakistan, the United States, India and Australia. Khan described spending three years in Pakistan from 1999 developing new music informed by Black funk principles, including the live Islamabad recording “Agar.” Across these movements, his songs have brought together singer-songwriter structure, eastern melodic feeling, world-pop colour and studio craft.

Sydney Opera House

While studying at Sydney Film School, Khan saw the Sydney Opera House daily from the Harbour Bridge and began imagining a live project there. He later assembled a nine-piece band, wrote the material, rehearsed and secured the venue for what he described as the first live album at the Opera House by a Pakistani artist.

The orchestral project

His subsequent orchestral album fulfilled another long-held ambition. Khan wrote new songs, worked with Doug Emery and David Griffin on arrangements, directed the filming of the recording, and recorded with a full symphony orchestra—bringing original songs, orchestral scale and world-pop sensibility together.

Recognition reported in 2020

Several 2020 reports and interviews documented international chart attention. Khan stated that Ginoo had already reached Billboard, while the orchestral project was reported as moving from No. 7 to No. 1 on Billboard’s Classical Crossover chart. The same interview records his account that Like the River reached No. 1 on the ARIA charts. These are presented as reported achievements; the source links below lead to the original coverage.

Power Lines

Power Lines is the latest point on that path. Its songs draw together memory, distance, reinvention, love, separation and freedom—the human currents that have moved through Khan’s life across countries, studios and stages.

Early life & the making of a musician

Mahmood Khan’s relationship with music began early. In a 2020 interview, he recalled being drawn first to songs on television, then to piano and guitar at boarding school. A large record collection and an early fascination with how records were made led him toward songcraft, production and sound engineering.

From records to the studio

Khan has spoken of becoming increasingly drawn to artists who wrote their own material. Hearing the Bee Gees’ “Too Much Heaven”, he has said, made him want to understand how records were built—and eventually to become a record producer. That focus led him to Los Angeles, where he found work in a recording studio and developed practical experience as an engineer and producer.

Learning from Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

While developing his voice, Khan immersed himself in South Asian classical vocal traditions. He has named Bade Ghulam Ali Khan, Malika Pukhraj and Aziz Mian Qawwal among the artists he studied. Through a connection formed when Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan came to Los Angeles for a Peter Gabriel concert, Khan spent time in close proximity to the legendary vocalist, observing his dawn practice and absorbing Qawwali technique, discipline and riyaz.

That encounter was not an imitation exercise, but a deepening of his own musical vocabulary. The influence remains part of the emotional and vocal language that connects his work to his Pakistani heritage.

Writing across places

His work has unfolded between Pakistan, the United States, India and Australia. In a Daily Times interview, Khan described spending three years in Pakistan from 1999 developing new music informed by Black funk principles, including the live Islamabad recording “Agar.” Throughout these shifts of place, his songs have continued to bring together singer-songwriter structure, eastern melodic feeling, world-pop colour and studio craft.

Sydney Opera House & an orchestral vision

While studying at Sydney Film School, Khan saw the Sydney Opera House daily from the Harbour Bridge and began imagining a live project there. He later assembled a nine-piece band, wrote the material, rehearsed and secured the venue for what he described as the first live album at the Opera House by a Pakistani artist.

His subsequent orchestral album fulfilled another long-held ambition. Khan wrote new songs, worked with Doug Emery and David Griffin on arrangements, directed the filming of the recording, and recorded with a full symphony orchestra. He has described the project as a meeting of new original songs, orchestral scale and the world-pop sensibility at the heart of his music.

Recognition reported in 2020

Several 2020 reports and interviews documented a period of international chart attention. Khan stated that Ginoo had already reached Billboard, while the orchestral project was reported as moving from No. 7 to No. 1 on Billboard’s Classical Crossover chart. The same interview records his account that Like the River, released after the Sydney Opera House project, reached No. 1 on the ARIA charts. Contemporary reporting also covered the Billboard placement of Ginoo.

Power Lines

Power Lines is the latest point on that path. Its songs draw together memory, distance, reinvention, love, separation and freedom—the same human currents that have moved through Khan’s life across countries, studios and stages. It is not a retrospective. It is a new chapter shaped by the whole journey behind it.

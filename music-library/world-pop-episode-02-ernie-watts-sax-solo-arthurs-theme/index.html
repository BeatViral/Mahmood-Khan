The saxophone solo in Arthur's Theme: why one line can carry a song

One brief musical part becomes a reason to hear the entire arrangement differently: Ernie Watts's saxophone in Christopher Cross's Arthur's Theme.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

The instruction is simple: follow the saxophone

In the second WORLD POP production script, Mahmood Khan introduces Christopher Cross's 'Arthur's Theme (Best That You Can Do)' with a particular request: listeners should pay close attention to the saxophone solo. He describes its melodic character with the enthusiasm of someone who has spent a lifetime making and examining records.

This is a useful editorial choice because the instrumental line is not the song's title, chorus or lead vocal. It is a contribution that may initially seem secondary. Once pointed out, it can become the musical event around which a listener remembers the recording.

Who wrote and played what

The song appeared with the 1981 film Arthur. The American Film Institute identifies four writers: Christopher Cross, Burt Bacharach, Carole Bayer Sager and Peter Allen. Warner/Rhino's digital soundtrack credits identify Ernie Watts as saxophonist, David Hungate on bass, Jeff Porcaro on drums, Michael Omartian on keyboards and strings arrangement, and several notable studio musicians on guitar and percussion.

The exact credits matter because 'written with Burt Bacharach' describes songwriting, while 'the saxophone played by Ernie Watts' describes recorded performance. These are different contributions, and both help explain why a seemingly simple radio hit can contain so much musical personality.

Why a short part can change the memory of a whole record

One instrumental phrase can become a listener's emotional landmark. A solo can provide contrast against a sustained vocal line; it may enter in a different register or place a new timbre above familiar harmony. In a dense but controlled arrangement, a distinctive solo gives the ear a fresh point of focus.

Khan draws an analogy to noticing the bass part in a Paul Simon recording. The Episode 2 passage does not identify that comparison track by name, so this article does not assign one. The principle remains useful: record-makers know that a memorable bass or saxophone line may tell a story of its own.

The work hidden inside a few minutes

Elsewhere in the Episode 2 script, Khan reflects on the cleanliness and layering of the arrangement, suggesting the many small sonic details that must be balanced in a finished mix. That is an artistic observation, not evidence of precisely how many days or weeks the engineers spent mixing the record.

Listen first to the song as a whole and then once more for the saxophone alone. Where is it placed against the voice? How does its tone sit inside the arrangement? Does the musical energy rise without the arrangement becoming louder or denser? This kind of focused listening is the experience that WORLD POP is trying to share.

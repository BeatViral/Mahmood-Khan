What Mahmood Khan has said about recording with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

The documented recording relationship is more revealing than a résumé line: working in close quarters, listening to practice and creating new music together.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

A meeting that started with a recording

In a July 2020 interview with Saira Agha, Mahmood Khan described becoming fascinated with Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan's vocal approach while he was developing his own singing. He sent Nusrat recordings of his work and learned that the singer would be coming to Los Angeles to perform. Khan said he went to receive him at the airport, beginning a period of unusually close contact.

This account is important because it presents the collaboration as a meeting of musical interests rather than a purely commercial assignment. Khan was already working with recording technology and popular styles in the United States; Nusrat brought an extraordinary lifetime of South Asian vocal practice.

The temporary studio and the working environment

Khan told Daily Times that he and Nusrat spent months living in the same Santa Monica residence, where he assembled a studio to record material. He also recalled recording some songs at the studio of Iranian artist Morteza Barjesteh, who had offered the space. These are the specific places and working circumstances that deserve documenting in any serious account of the album.

His recollections include plans to make more recordings than circumstances allowed. The resulting collaboration has been described by Khan as a multi-million-selling album; precise sales, release versions and production credits should continue to be supported by original label documentation where available.

What listening to practice taught him

In the August 2020 interview, Khan described sitting close by when Nusrat practised his voice in the early morning. He connected those observations to an interest in vocal discipline, phrasing and classical South Asian singing. That learning also belongs alongside the lessons he took from Western vocal coaches and from American studio work.

The distinction is important. Qawwali is a devotional and poetic tradition, not merely a set of impressive vocal techniques. A musician working across cultures can learn about expression, breath and improvisation while also recognising the context and people from which that knowledge comes.

A fuller session history is still possible

The interviews preserve Khan's memories, but they are not substitutes for session reels, dated tape boxes, production credits or additional witness accounts. Those materials could establish exactly which songs were recorded in which studio, who played and sang, and what arrangements developed during the sessions.

For WORLD POP, this is potentially a powerful series of conversations about musical inheritance: what happens when a producer shaped by American funk, R&B and pop spends months working with one of South Asia's most influential singers? The published interviews give us a basis for asking that question without inventing private dialogue.

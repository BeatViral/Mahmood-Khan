Why studio monitors are usually placed in a triangle

Equal distances and symmetrical positioning help create a stable stereo listening picture.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

Understand the principle

Two studio monitors can create an apparent soundstage between them, but their placement strongly affects that illusion. A common starting point is an equilateral triangle with the listener at one corner and the speakers at the others, aimed toward the listening position. The equal paths help sound from left and right reach the listener under similar conditions.

Why a listener notices

Room geometry also matters. A speaker close to one wall and far from another can experience different reflections and bass response from its partner. Moving the chair a modest distance may change the balance you hear, even though the audio files and monitors remain unchanged.

A practical test

Place two speakers at matched height and distance. Listen to a centrally panned vocal, then shift your chair 30 centimetres to one side. Does the voice seem to move toward one speaker? Restore the symmetrical position and test a carefully chosen recording with clear left-to-right instrument placement.

Choose by listening

The triangle is a starting geometry, not a guarantee of accuracy. Room modes, speaker directivity and desk reflections also affect results. Measuring and listening in the actual space is more useful than assuming an expensive monitor will defeat poor placement.

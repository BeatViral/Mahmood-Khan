David Foster's St. Elmo's Fire: the instrumental love theme behind the atmosphere

The atmospheric instrumental 'Love Theme from St. Elmo's Fire' shows Foster as a composer, performer and arranger, not just a producer behind pop hits.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

A clue in the Episode 2 sequence

The WORLD POP Episode 2 project script moves from Burt Bacharach to David Foster, and includes a reference to the instrumental theme from the 1985 film St. Elmo's Fire. It belongs in the same listening journey as Neil Diamond's 'Heartlight' because Foster's work ranges from the keyboard parts inside songs to complete instrumental compositions.

A basic identification matters here. The instrumental 'Love Theme from St. Elmo's Fire' is not the same recording as John Parr's vocal hit 'St. Elmo's Fire (Man in Motion)'. They share a film association, but different compositions and credits should not be folded into one.

The credited composition

The official Atlantic Records audio listing credits David Foster as composer, keyboardist and producer of the instrumental Love Theme. The released recording is also associated with engineer and mixer Humberto Gatica and guitarist Michael Landau. MusicBrainz records Foster's arrangement credit and the production collaboration with Gatica.

Those credits show several layers of authorship. Foster composed and arranged the musical material; the instrumental performers and production team shaped how it was recorded and heard. The theme has no lead lyric to explain its emotional direction. It depends on melody, tone and the pace at which musical ideas enter.

Why instrumental film music belongs in a pop programme

A film theme may be attached to characters, settings and visual memories, but it can also live independently as a recording. Some listeners may not know the scenes when they hear it. They still encounter a sequence of musical decisions that can create space, intimacy or anticipation.

That is the connection to John Barry's The Persuaders! theme in this WORLD POP edition. These composers were not making the same work, but both used recorded instrumental music to establish atmosphere. A songwriter listening to a theme can learn how much narrative information a melody and arrangement convey before anyone sings.

Follow one musical layer at a time

Listen first to the instrumental melody. On another pass, notice the keyboard tone, the guitar's relationship to the main idea, and the feeling of space created by the production. Are the harmonic changes supporting an emotional arrival, or keeping the listener suspended? What would change if a voice entered over the same arrangement?

The official release and catalogue credits are the historical basis for these observations. We have not asserted that the finished Episode 2 broadcast included an extended analysis of each instrument, only that the theme was part of the writer's planned listening sequence. The purpose here is to take that moment further with a sourced companion essay.

Listening without reputation: why the record mattered more than the star

What happens when you encounter a song before you know who sings it, how famous they are, or which format is considered prestigious?

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

The unexpected advantage of not knowing the artist

One of the most distinctive passages in Mahmood Khan's Episode 2 production notes recalls his childhood listening in Pakistan. He inherited a turntable and hundreds of vinyl records from an aunt in Switzerland, then sorted them into personal favourites without knowing which performers were celebrated, successful or culturally fashionable.

He describes the experience as pure listening without prejudice. It is a first-person memory, not a claim to have carried out a controlled experiment. The idea is nevertheless powerful: the listener's emotional relationship to a record could begin before anyone else supplied a ranking or reputation.

Expectations influence what is heard

A 2022 study on vinyl and digital listening reported that expectations about the format influenced people's post-listening evaluations. Participants were told they would hear vinyl or MP3 recordings, while the actual format was sometimes different. The study concluded that the expectation surrounding a format could affect perceived quality.

This does not establish that Khan's childhood judgements were free from every influence. It simply makes the question about preconceptions worth asking. A familiar artist name, an expensive turntable, an admired producer or a prestigious record label can become part of what a listener thinks they are hearing.

Memory and recognition are separate from names

Music psychologists have also studied recognition without identification: listeners can recognise a fragment as familiar without being able to name the tune. Work by Bogdan Kostic and Anne Cleary documented this distinction across multiple experiments, including altered rhythmic presentations of music.

That research speaks to another side of Khan's recollection. A record may become intimately familiar through repeated listening even when the listener knows almost nothing about the artist. Recognition can grow from the sound itself, well before that sound has a public identity for the listener.

An exercise in hearing first

Ask someone to play you a recording you have never heard, without announcing the artist, date, genre or critical reputation. Make a few notes about what the melody, performance and production do to you. Only afterward reveal the names, session credits and history. Notice which observations stay the same and which become more interesting in light of the new context.

This is not an argument for keeping music anonymous forever. The people who create a record deserve recognition, and cultural origins deserve attention. The useful sequence is to hear first, then learn more. The Episode 2 story introduces that possibility; research on expectation and recognition gives listeners a way to explore it critically.

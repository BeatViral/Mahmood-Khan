Flying hours on the microphone: how recording develops a singer

A microphone is not only a tool for capturing a performance. Recording and listening back can become part of learning how to perform.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

Why 'flying hours' is a useful idea

In aviation, flying hours describe experience gained by actually being in the air. Mahmood Khan uses the expression in a musical sense: singers develop valuable experience by spending time with the microphone, making recordings and hearing their own voices come back through speakers.

A singer may feel entirely confident in a room and be surprised by the recorded result. The microphone captures small changes in distance, breath, articulation and dynamics. The recording offers a different perspective from the one we experience while singing.

Record, listen, notice, repeat

The useful cycle is simple. Sing a passage. Record it. Listen without trying to defend the performance. Notice what you intended to express and what the recording actually communicates. Then sing it again with one adjustment in mind.

The adjustment need not be dramatic. It could be the timing of a consonant, the end of a phrase, the relationship between a quiet opening and a stronger finish, or the amount of space left before the next line. Repetition becomes more productive when it is connected to attentive listening.

The equipment is part of the experience

Microphones respond differently to proximity, tone and movement. Headphones can make a voice feel unusually immediate. Room acoustics and monitoring levels also affect how a singer judges a take. Understanding these conditions is part of becoming comfortable in a recording environment.

Expensive equipment is not the starting requirement for this process. What matters first is a recording that lets the singer hear differences reliably, and a practical habit of returning to the material.

Technique, expression and influence

For Mahmood Khan, vocal learning also connects with the musical examples that shaped his own listening, including Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, and with the work of vocal teachers such as Seth Riggs. These are influences and points of reference, not a claim that one method is right for every singer.

The larger point is experiential: confidence behind a microphone comes from time spent using one. Each take offers a small piece of information. Over time, those observations can become instinct.

How Like the River was developed and recorded at Sydney Opera House

The story of Like the River is about building a live band and completing a recording long before the chart result.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

The work preceded the venue

Khan's accounts of Like the River begin well before the concert. In the July 2020 Daily Times interview, he described spending two years developing an Australian band, writing songs and improving his singing. In the August interview he recalled months of rehearsals and the difficulty of presenting a credible proposal to Sydney Opera House while still relatively unfamiliar to Australian audiences.

The variation in the number of musicians and exact preparation period between retrospective interviews is worth recording rather than flattening. Both accounts agree on the central fact: the performance was the outcome of sustained preparation, not a spontaneous booking.

A live performance had to become a recording

Recording a concert is different from making a studio track where sections can be replaced repeatedly. The band, audience, stage acoustics and microphone decisions interact at the same moment. A live album must capture that event and later make musical sense through editing, mixing and mastering.

Khan told Daily Times that he listened back with uncertainty about whether the live recordings were strong enough to release. He identified Like the River as a promising song, completed mixing and mastering, and took it to MGM Distribution. The account is particularly revealing because it describes professional doubt and practical follow-through after an ambitious live performance.

Chart success followed promotion

The single went to No. 1 on the ARIA Physical Singles chart. That exact chart category matters: a chart achievement is strongest when its title, format and week can be identified, rather than blurred into an unspecific claim about every Australian pop chart.

Khan credits local promotion, community radio and television with helping the release reach listeners. That part of the story is relevant to current experiments with WORLD POP and digital discovery: excellent recordings still need a route into people's attention.

The enduring creative question

It is possible to tell this story as a list of milestones. But the more useful questions are about process. Why did the band sound the way it did? Which arrangements were written for the concert? How were acoustic and electronic elements balanced? What changed between the stage and the final master?

The live video and original mixes can help answer those questions. An archive that pairs verified recordings with the artist's first-person interviews will teach listeners far more than simply repeating the chart achievement.

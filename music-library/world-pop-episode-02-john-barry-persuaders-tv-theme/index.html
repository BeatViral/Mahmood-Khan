John Barry's The Persuaders!: a complete musical world before the dialogue

A 1971 TV theme makes a case for a complete musical identity built from atmosphere, instruments and dramatic timing.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

Why the choice belongs in WORLD POP

Mahmood Khan's Episode 2 planning notes specifically choose John Barry's theme for The Persuaders! as the entry point into a sequence of film composers, songwriters and producers. He has also described it in project correspondence as an especially cherished composition. It is not a casual reference among dozens of records; it is a deliberate listening choice.

The programme sequence later moves toward Burt Bacharach, David Foster and Christopher Cross. Those musicians did not all work on The Persuaders! theme. The relationship here is conceptual: how music written for a screen can carry an emotional identity strong enough to be enjoyed apart from the film or television programme.

John Barry in 1971

Barry composed the opening theme for The Persuaders!, a 1971 television series starring Roger Moore and Tony Curtis. Classic FM identifies the Moog synthesizer as part of the theme's sound. York Civic Trust's retrospective on Barry notes his wider habit of combining classical, jazz and pop influences with electric instruments and unusual textures.

The sound of a synthesizer was not simply a gimmick added to an otherwise traditional orchestra. Placed alongside orchestral writing, it became another source of colour and atmosphere. That mixture helps explain why the theme can sound simultaneously tied to its period and strikingly cinematic when heard now.

The art of creating a world in seconds

A television title theme has to establish a scene almost immediately. The composer has limited time to suggest mystery, pace, humour, danger or glamour. Harmony, timbre and rhythmic emphasis must do much of the storytelling before a character speaks. Barry's theme provides a useful example of that compressed musical task.

For a listener, the best experiment is not to chase technical vocabulary first. Hear the theme once as pure music. Then listen again for the contrast between its keyboard colour and its orchestral lines. Notice how a recurring motif can build identity even when no lyric explains what is happening.

The path toward Bacharach

John Barry and Burt Bacharach each moved between popular idioms and dramatic composition, but they did so in different ways. WORLD POP's transition from Barry to Bacharach asks listeners to compare musical functions, not to conflate their credits. Barry's theme demonstrates world-building without a lyric; Bacharach's songs demonstrate the meeting of melodic writing, vocals and imaginative arrangements.

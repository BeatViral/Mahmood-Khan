From an inherited record collection to Los Angeles studios

What Saira Agha's 2020 interviews reveal about the musical influences and practical apprenticeship behind Mahmood Khan's sound.

WORLD POP music knowledge library · November 2026

AI-assisted research note · Sources linked below · Not a broadcast transcript or a first-person account by Mahmood Khan

Early listening was an education

In two extended interviews with Saira Agha for Daily Times in 2020, Mahmood Khan returned to an unusual part of his childhood: learning instruments at boarding school while listening to Western popular recordings inherited from an aunt who lived in Switzerland. He described access to pianos, a teacher who played guitar as well as piano, and a record collection that brought Motown and internationally popular singers into his immediate world.

The important detail is not simply that he heard records from abroad. He began distinguishing between performers who interpreted songs and singer-songwriters who appeared to be shaping their own musical identities. That distinction helped form an ambition to become both a writer and a producer. In the August interview he singled out hearing the Bee Gees' Too Much Heaven as a particularly formative event.

From liking records to asking how they were constructed

Khan described becoming interested in the architecture of recordings: how harmonies were stacked, how instruments were placed, how a vocal could be both recognisable and carefully produced. He says that fascination led him to read sound-engineering material long before he had an established place in the industry.

This is a useful contrast to the notion that musical taste and technical skill are unrelated. Listening provided the questions; the studio became a place to test the answers. The interviews document a long process rather than a sudden transformation into a professional producer.

An apprenticeship among American Black music

After college in Lahore, Khan travelled to Los Angeles and found work as a second engineer at a Black music production house. In the July 2020 interview he described spending roughly fourteen years in that environment developing skills in recording, production and writing. His later references to funk, hip-hop, R&B and Motown are grounded in this actual studio experience as well as in the records he heard growing up.

This background matters when listening to his subsequent work. Musical influence is not just a list of admired artists: it can shape how a songwriter approaches bass movement, rhythm, phrasing and the relationship between a vocal and its backing tracks.

Why the original interviews matter

The two interviews offer more than a biography. They contain the artist's own account of how records taught him to think, why he sought out recording technology and how Los Angeles experience changed the kind of music he was able to make. Where the interviews give slightly different ages for childhood events, the archive should preserve the source wording rather than invent an artificially precise timeline.

The next layer of this history should come from recorded conversations and archival evidence: the full Journey of a New Sound documentary, dated studio photographs, surviving early recordings and interviews with collaborators. Those could bring the years between listening to records and producing records into sharper focus.
